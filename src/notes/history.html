A History of Databases

From Clay Tablets to Vector Embeddings

How humanity's oldest problem — keeping reliable records — became the science of structuring, storing, and retrieving information, and how that science is being reshaped again in the age of AI.

1. Before Computers: The Administrative Origins of Data

Long before anyone spoke of "databases," human societies were already solving the database problem: how to record facts durably, organize them so they could be found again, and trust that they were accurate. That problem is as old as large-scale administration itself, and its first solutions were built not from silicon but from clay, reed, and stone.

Mesopotamia: The First Recordkeepers

In the city-states of ancient Sumer, roughly five thousand years ago, temple and palace administrators faced a very modern problem: tracking grain deliveries, herd counts, land allotments, and tax obligations across a growing bureaucracy. Their answer was cuneiform writing pressed into wet clay tablets with a reed stylus, producing durable, tamper-resistant records once dried or fired. These tablets were not free-form prose; they followed rigid, repeated formats — a quantity, a commodity, a person or institution responsible, a date, a place. That regularity is the essence of a data record: fixed fields, consistent structure, and a predictable way to encode a fact so that someone else, later, could decode it correctly.

Sumerian and later Babylonian archives went further, physically organizing tablets by subject in labeled baskets and niches within temple storerooms — an early form of indexing. Some administrative tablets were even structured in tabular form, with rows and columns separated by incised lines, centuries before anyone imagined a spreadsheet.

Egypt: Registries, Censuses, and the Bureaucratic State

Ancient Egypt built one of history's most sophisticated bureaucracies on top of papyrus rather than clay. Scribes maintained land registers to support Nile flood-based taxation, grain-storage ledgers to buffer against famine, and labor rosters for state building projects such as the pyramids and irrigation works. Papyrus was lighter and more compact than clay, which allowed longer, cross-referenced documents and something closer to a filing system: scrolls stored in labeled jars and archive rooms, retrievable by category. The Egyptian state also pioneered periodic censuses, an early example of systematic, repeated data collection used directly to inform governance and taxation policy — a practice that would not be formalized again at scale until early modern Europe.

Libraries as Structured Knowledge Stores

The impulse to organize information matured further in institutions such as the Library of Alexandria, founded in the third century BCE, which is believed to have used cataloguing systems — most notably Callimachus's Pinakes — to classify works by subject and author across what may have been hundreds of thousands of scrolls. This was, in effect, an early metadata system: a catalogue record standing in for the full document, allowing a reader to search and retrieve without physically browsing every shelf. The core ideas that define a database today — structured records, controlled vocabularies, indexes, and retrieval by attribute rather than by brute-force search — were all present in embryonic form well over two thousand years before the first computer.

2. Mechanizing the Record: Punch Cards and Tabulation

The next major leap did not come from a computer either, but from the pressure of a single overwhelming administrative task. The 1880 United States Census took nearly eight years to tabulate by hand, and officials feared the 1890 census, given population growth, would not be finished before the next one began.

Herman Hollerith, an engineer working with the Census Bureau, solved this with punched cards and an electromechanical tabulating machine that could count and sort records by detecting holes with electrical contacts. Each card represented one person or household, with holes at fixed positions encoding attributes such as age, occupation, and marital status. This was, functionally, the first mechanized record format with fixed fields and machine-readable structure. The 1890 census was completed in about a year, and Hollerith's Tabulating Machine Company later merged with several others to become, in 1924, the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company's successor: IBM.

For the next several decades, punch cards and magnetic tape were the primary medium for business data. Records were processed in batches: a deck of cards or a tape reel was read sequentially from start to finish to update a master file. There was no concept of a "query" in the modern sense — retrieving a single record meant physically locating a card or scanning an entire tape. This sequential, file-based model would remain the norm in business computing well into the 1960s.

3. The First True Databases: Hierarchical and Network Models

The term "database" itself emerged in the early 1960s, as computers began storing data on magnetic disks rather than tape. Disks allowed random access — jumping directly to a record instead of reading everything before it — which made it possible, for the first time, to design software specifically for managing shared, queryable collections of data rather than single-purpose files.

IBM's IMS and the Hierarchical Model

In 1966, IBM began developing what became the Information Management System (IMS), commissioned to track the enormous bill-of-materials data for the Apollo space program, which involved millions of individual components. IMS organized data as a strict hierarchy of parent and child records, similar to a tree or an org chart. This worked well for structured, predictable relationships like a parts list, but it forced designers to decide the access pattern in advance, since navigating the tree in an unanticipated direction was slow or impossible. Remarkably, IMS is still in active use in banking and airline systems today, more than half a century later.

CODASYL and the Network Model

In parallel, the Conference on Data Systems Languages (CODASYL), the same industry group that had produced COBOL, developed the network model, championed by Charles Bachman. Rather than a strict tree, records could have multiple parents, forming a flexible graph of relationships. Bachman's earlier work, the Integrated Data Store (IDS) built at General Electric in 1963–64, is often considered the first true database management system, and its design directly shaped the CODASYL standard. Bachman received the Turing Award in 1973, computing's highest honor, for this work.

Both hierarchical and network databases were powerful but demanding. Programmers had to navigate data structures manually, one link or pointer at a time, and any change to the underlying schema could break every application built on top of it. Querying data required deep knowledge of exactly how it was physically stored. Something simpler and more mathematically grounded was needed.

4. Edgar Codd and the Relational Revolution

In 1970, Edgar F. Codd, a British mathematician working at IBM's San Jose research laboratory, published a paper titled A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks. It is one of the most consequential papers in the history of computer science.

Codd's central insight was to separate the logical structure of data from its physical storage. Instead of forcing users to navigate pointers and trees, he proposed representing all data simply as tables — relations, in mathematical terms — made up of rows and columns, grounded in set theory and predicate logic. Users could then ask questions about the data ("which employees earn more than their manager?") without knowing anything about how it was physically stored or linked. The database system itself would figure out the most efficient way to answer the question.

This was a radical simplification, and it introduced ideas that remain foundational today:

  • Tables and tuples — data organized into relations, each row a tuple of attribute values.
  • Data independence — applications insulated from changes in physical storage layout.
  • Declarative querying — describing what data is wanted, not how to fetch it.
  • Normalization — a formal discipline for structuring tables to minimize redundancy and avoid update anomalies, later refined by Codd and others into first, second, and third normal form.

IBM was initially reluctant to build a product around Codd's ideas because it risked cannibalizing IMS revenue, but a group of IBM researchers pursued a prototype anyway. Meanwhile, at the University of California, Berkeley, Michael Stonebraker and Eugene Wong began the Ingres project, another influential early relational system, whose lineage later fed into Sybase, Microsoft SQL Server, and PostgreSQL.

System R and the Birth of SQL

IBM's own relational prototype, System R, built through the mid-1970s, proved that relational databases could be made fast enough for real use, and it introduced a query language called SEQUEL — Structured English Query Language — designed by Donald Chamberlin and Raymond Boyce to make Codd's relational algebra accessible to ordinary programmers using English-like syntax. Renamed SQL for trademark reasons, it became, and remains, the dominant language for interacting with relational data.

Ironically, the company that first brought a relational database to market was not IBM but a small startup founded in 1977 by Larry Ellison, Bob Miner, and Ed Oates, originally called Software Development Laboratories. Having read Codd's paper and an early description of System R, they raced ahead of IBM and shipped Oracle, the first commercially available SQL relational database, in 1979 — two years before IBM's own commercial product, SQL/DS, followed by DB2 in 1983. Codd's theoretical breakthrough had become a multi-billion-dollar industry within a decade.

5. Relational Databases Take Over the Enterprise

Through the 1980s and 1990s, relational database management systems (RDBMSs) became the backbone of business computing. Oracle, IBM DB2, Sybase, and later Microsoft SQL Server (built initially on Sybase technology) competed for the enterprise market, while academic and open-source efforts produced systems still in wide use today: PostgreSQL, which traces its lineage to Stonebraker's Berkeley POSTGRES project of the mid-1980s, and MySQL, released in 1995, which became the default database for the web alongside Linux, Apache, and PHP — the "LAMP stack."

This era also formalized the theory underpinning transactional reliability, most notably the ACID properties — Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, and Durability — articulated by researchers including Jim Gray, another Turing Award winner, whose work on transaction processing made it possible to trust that a bank transfer, an airline booking, or an inventory update would either complete fully or not at all, even in the face of crashes or concurrent access. By the late 1990s, the relational model, SQL, and ACID transactions were simply how serious data was managed, and it was hard to imagine an alternative gaining serious traction.

6. Cracks in the Model: The Rise of NoSQL

That changed with the internet's growth in scale during the 2000s. Companies like Google, Amazon, and later Facebook found themselves managing data at a volume and velocity — billions of users, globally distributed, constantly written and read — that strained the assumptions of traditional relational systems, which were generally designed to run on a single powerful machine and scale "up" rather than "out" across many cheap machines.

Two influential papers reshaped the field. Google's 2006 paper on Bigtable described a distributed storage system for structured data spanning thousands of commodity servers, built to support services like Google Search and Google Maps. Amazon's 2007 paper on Dynamo described a highly available key-value store designed to keep the shopping cart working even during network partitions or server failures, prioritizing availability over strict consistency — a trade-off formalized by computer scientist Eric Brewer as the CAP theorem, which holds that a distributed system can guarantee at most two of Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance at once.

These ideas fed a wave of new, non-relational — "NoSQL" — databases, each optimized for a different shape of problem rather than the general-purpose relational table:

  • Key-value stores (Redis, Riak, DynamoDB) — extremely fast lookups by a single key, ideal for caching and session data.
  • Document databases (MongoDB, CouchDB) — flexible, schema-less JSON-like documents, popular for rapidly evolving web application data.
  • Column-family stores (Cassandra, HBase, Bigtable) — built for massive write throughput across distributed clusters.
  • Graph databases (Neo4j) — optimized for traversing dense networks of relationships, such as social graphs or fraud-detection networks.

NoSQL systems generally traded some of relational databases' strong consistency and rigid schemas for horizontal scalability and developer flexibility. By the 2010s, most large organizations were running a mix of relational and non-relational systems, each chosen for the specific workload it served best — a shift often described as "polyglot persistence."

NewSQL and the Cloud

The following decade produced a partial synthesis: "NewSQL" systems such as Google Spanner, CockroachDB, and Amazon Aurora attempted to combine the horizontal scalability of NoSQL with the strong consistency and SQL interface of the relational world. Meanwhile, the database itself increasingly became a service rather than software one installed and managed — Amazon RDS, Google Cloud SQL, Snowflake, and similar cloud-native, fully managed platforms let organizations rent elastic, auto-scaling data infrastructure rather than operating it themselves, fundamentally changing who needed deep database administration expertise at all.

7. The AI Era: Vectors, Embeddings, and Retrieval

The rise of large language models and generative AI in the 2020s introduced a new kind of data to manage: not rows of structured attributes, but high-dimensional vector embeddings — numerical representations that capture the semantic meaning of text, images, audio, or other unstructured content. Two pieces of content with similar meaning end up as vectors that are mathematically close together, even if they share no words in common.

Finding the "nearest" vectors to a query among millions or billions of candidates is a fundamentally different problem from looking up an exact key or filtering rows by an equality condition, and traditional relational indexes are poorly suited to it. This has driven the emergence of a new category: vector databases and vector search engines — such as Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, and Qdrant — purpose-built around approximate nearest-neighbor search algorithms like HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds). At the same time, established databases have absorbed this capability directly rather than ceding the ground entirely: PostgreSQL gained vector search through the pgvector extension, and Elasticsearch, MongoDB, and Redis have all added native vector indexing.

This capability underpins retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), now one of the most common architectures in production AI systems, in which a language model's answer is grounded by first retrieving relevant passages from a vector database — letting a general-purpose model reason over an organization's private, current documents rather than only its training data. AI has also begun reshaping how people interact with conventional databases: natural-language-to-SQL tools now let non-technical users query structured data conversationally, and database systems themselves increasingly use machine learning internally, for tasks like predicting query performance, automatically tuning indexes, and detecting anomalies — a research direction sometimes called "self-driving" or autonomous databases.

In a real sense, the field has come full circle. Five thousand years ago, the challenge was encoding meaning into a durable, structured symbol — a mark in clay standing for a measure of grain. Today's challenge is encoding meaning into a different kind of structured symbol — a vector of numbers standing for a concept — so that it, too, can be reliably stored, indexed, and retrieved. The medium has changed completely. The underlying problem has not.

8. Timeline at a Glance

  • ~3200 BCE — Cuneiform record-keeping on clay tablets in Sumer.
  • ~2500–1500 BCE — Egyptian papyrus registries, censuses, and archives.
  • 3rd c. BCE — Library of Alexandria and Callimachus's Pinakes catalogue.
  • 1890 — Hollerith's punched-card tabulator processes the U.S. Census.
  • 1960s — Random-access disks enable the first true database systems.
  • 1966 — IBM begins developing IMS (hierarchical model) for Apollo.
  • 1963–1973 — Bachman's IDS and the CODASYL network model.
  • 1970 — Edgar Codd publishes the relational model.
  • 1974–1979 — IBM's System R and Chamberlin/Boyce's SQL; Oracle ships the first commercial SQL RDBMS.
  • 1980s–1990s — Enterprise RDBMS era: DB2, Sybase, SQL Server, PostgreSQL, MySQL.
  • 2006–2007 — Google Bigtable and Amazon Dynamo papers spark the NoSQL movement.
  • 2010s — Cloud-managed databases and NewSQL systems combine scale with consistency.
  • 2020s — Vector databases and retrieval-augmented generation for AI applications.

Every era's database reflects what that era most needed to remember and how fast it needed to find it again. Sumerian scribes needed to prove a grain debt was paid. Cold War-era programmers needed to track a spacecraft's ten million parts. Web-era engineers needed a page to load in milliseconds for a billion users at once. Today's systems need to retrieve not just facts, but meaning. The tools keep changing; the underlying human project — building trustworthy memory at scale — does not.
